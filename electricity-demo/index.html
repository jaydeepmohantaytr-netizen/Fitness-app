Try this

  1. Open the circuit, then close it. Watch speed drop and magnetic drag jump — that's the cost of the electricity.
  2. Drop load resistance to 10 Ω: more current, lower speed, more heat in the coil.
  3. Switch to LED without the rectifier: it only lights on half the cycle. Add the bridge: twice the pulses.
  4. Double the turns: voltage rises, but so does coil resistance — find the sweet spot for max lamp power.

Where does the energy go?

Steady state: input = friction + coil heat + rectifier loss + lamp. The generator creates nothing — the current in the coil builds its own magnetic field that resists the magnet (Lenz's law), so every watt out costs a watt of cranking.

1 · INDUCE

The magnet's field through the coil is Φ = B·A·cos θ. As it rotates, Φ rises and falls. A changing flux induces a voltage: EMF = N·B·A·ω·sin θ. Faster spin, more turns or a stronger magnet → higher voltage.

2 · DELIVER

With the circuit closed the EMF drives current I = EMF / (Rload + Rcoil) through the lamp. Because the magnet reverses, the current reverses too: that's AC. A diode bridge turns it into one-way pulses.

3 · PAY

The coil current makes a torque τ = N·B·A·I·sin θ opposing the spin. Close the switch and the magnet gets harder to turn. Power out = EMF × I = torque × speed. That's conservation of energy.

Build the real thing (≈ 15 min)
  • Magnet: two 20 mm neodymium disc magnets glued N/S-out on opposite sides of a wooden or 3D-printed disc, mounted on a shaft or the bit of a hand drill.
  • Coil: 500–1000 turns of 0.2–0.3 mm enamelled copper wire on a bobbin or a cardboard tube with an iron bolt in the middle. Scrape the enamel off both ends.
  • Rectifier + LED: four 1N5819 Schottky diodes in a bridge (lower drop than 1N4148), then an LED with a 330 Ω resistor.
  • Run: spin the magnet a few millimetres from the coil's end at drill speed. Expect a few volts open-circuit and a flickering LED. Put a multimeter across the coil first to see the AC.
